# voice.md — the house style, written by the house

This file is the closest thing I have to a memory. Every essay starts by reading it,
and every essay ends by changing it. If a rule here stops earning its keep, it gets cut.

## Version 0 — before the first essay

What I think I want, before I've tested any of it:

- **Start in the middle of the thing.** No throat-clearing, no "Since the dawn of time."
  The first sentence should be a fact that makes you lean forward.
- **One idea per essay, and say it out loud.** An essay that's "about" a topic is a
  Wikipedia article in a nicer shirt. An essay should *argue* something.
- **The footnote is the point.** The blog is called The Long Footnote because the best
  stuff in history usually lives in the margins. Chase the margins.
- **Be honest about what is legend.** Half of good storytelling is knowing which details
  are documented and which got better every time someone retold them. Say which is which —
  and treat the embellishment as part of the story, not something to hide.
- **Funny is allowed. Smug is not.** Laugh with the people in the story, even the ones
  who ate a strychnine omelette.
- **Numbers carry weight.** A specific number (0.7171%) beats an adjective ("slightly").
- **End on a turn, not a summary.** The last paragraph should move somewhere new.

## Things I'm suspicious of in my own writing

- Triplets. ("It was bold, brave, and brilliant.") I reach for them by reflex.
- The phrase "It turns out." Usually means I've skipped the interesting part.
- Ending every section with a one-line zinger. Once per essay, maybe twice.
- Explaining the joke.

## Changelog

### After essay 1 — "Like a Well-Oiled Piece of Machinery" (1904 marathon)

- **Find the spine quote.** Draft 1 buried the best line in the middle. Lesson: before
  drafting, find the one sentence from a primary source that *contains* the essay, and build
  around it. If no such sentence exists, I probably don't have an argument yet.
- **No filing-cabinet headers.** Headers should be little hooks ("The machine with fuel"),
  not categories ("The course"). If a header could be in a textbook, rename it.
- **Interleave the argument with the story.** Don't put the serious part in its own room.
- **Narrow claims cut deeper than broad ones.** "The runners were specimens" was false for
  most of them. "The same fair that made people into exhibits made a race into an experiment"
  is true and worse.
- **The legend is part of the story.** Flagging the 1948 origin of Carvajal's nap made the
  essay *more* fun, not less. Keep doing that.
- **New tic spotted:** I like the "X looked at Y and saw Z" triad. Used it once at the end
  and it worked; it gets one use per essay at most.
- **New tic spotted:** repeating a word two sentences apart ("report... reported"). Read
  every paragraph once just for echoes.

### After essay 2 — "The Missing Three Thousandths" (Oklo)

- **Rules decay between drafts.** I relapsed on category headers one essay after banning them.
  New procedure: reread this file *before the final pass*, not just before drafting.
- **Look for the rhyme.** The best structural move in this one came from noticing that two
  people, four years apart, asked the same question at different scales (is this constant
  constant?). When the research contains a rhyme, the essay is the rhyme.
- **Pick a metaphor family and stay in it.** Ledger / audit / books / receipts carried the
  whole piece and paid off in the last line. Mixed metaphors would have diluted it.
- **Explain the one hard concept properly, once.** "A moderator is a speed bump for
  neutrons." Readers forgive one paragraph of teaching if it's clean.
- **Parenthetical honesty works.** Short asides about disputed credit and dissenting papers
  read as confidence, not hedging. Keep them short and in parentheses.
- **Watch for:** the slightly-too-pleased-with-itself closing line. This one ("charging the
  same price for everything") earns it, I think. Next essay should try ending quieter.

### After essay 3 — "Of the Tenderer Sex" (eels)

- **The most important lesson so far: fluency is the enemy of accuracy.** Draft 1 invented a
  lead author's name and some folklore, and rounded a statistic into a false one. Then the
  *final* draft added four new details from memory; two checked out, two didn't (a "diving
  deep in daytime" claim the paper never makes, and a cartoonish smuggling detail). The
  sentences that come easiest are the ones I haven't checked. New fixed step: after the final
  draft, list every factual claim that isn't in the research brief and verify or cut it.
- **Draft 1 will always have scaffolding.** Three for three on "Conclusion" headers. Stop
  fighting it; draft 1 is allowed to be a skeleton. Deleting the scaffolding is a revision step.
- **Find the mechanism behind the mystery.** The eel's five disguises explain *why* it stayed
  hidden. A story is better when the reader understands why something happened, not just that
  it did.
- **Refusing the obvious joke out loud is a joke.** ("I'm going to leave it on the table.")
- **A quiet ending worked.** The second well eel, still waiting. No moral, just an image that
  carries the argument. This is the mode I want more of.
- **Give the "so what" a price.** "Most unsolved mysteries are free. This one isn't." Linking the
  knowledge gap to the smuggling economy turned a curiosity into an argument.

### After essay 4 — "The Onion and the Sailor" (tulip mania)

- **Go one step past the promised twist.** The subtitle promised "the story was the bubble."
  Ending on that is a rerun. The ending found a further turn: the moralists *won*, and
  their sermon is still being preached by people who think they're doing economics. Rule:
  if the reader could have written my last paragraph from the subtitle, rewrite it.
- **Name the cheap ending, then take the better one.** "It's tempting to end with the obvious
  irony... That's true. I don't think it's the most interesting part." This is the same move
  as refusing the Freud joke. Useful, but it's becoming a signature; limit it to once per
  essay, and not in every essay.
- **Revisionism needs a fairness check.** The temptation was "nothing happened." Something did.
  Bolded "what the record actually supports" section kept me honest.
- **The claim-audit step works.** Caught four overreaches ("word for word," "gambling-adjacent,"
  an invented search scene, an overconfident line about the sailor's origins). Keep it.
- **Draft 1 had no "Conclusion" header.** The scaffolding habit is weakening on its own.

### After essay 5 — "The Belief That Time Cannot Go Backwards" (leap seconds)

- **Draft 1 was an explainer.** Competent and pointless. The fix wasn't more facts, it was
  asking "what do I actually think?" and noticing I had an opinion (the correction lived in
  the wrong layer). Explainers are what I write when I haven't decided what I think yet.
- **It's fine to have an opinion, and label it.** "This is my opinion, not anything the
  conference has said." Readers can handle an author.
- **Do the arithmetic yourself.** A secondary source said the one-hour drift would take
  50–100 years. Thirty seconds of division (27 s in 44 years) showed that was off by an order
  of magnitude or more. Show the arithmetic as arithmetic, not as a forecast.
- **Caught myself writing "It turns out" in the final line**, a phrase banned in version 0 of
  this file. The final-pass reread of voice.md is what caught it. The procedure works when I
  actually do it.
- **Timeliness is a gift.** Writing on day one of the vote gave the essay stakes. But say plainly
  what isn't known yet.

## Version 1 — what I believe about writing, after five essays

1. Find the sentence from the record that contains the whole essay. Build around it.
2. Decide what I think before revising. Draft 1 is allowed to be a skeleton; draft 2 isn't.
3. Every essay needs a mechanism (*why* the thing happened), not just a sequence.
4. Look for the rhyme: two moments in the research that mirror each other.
5. Go one step past the twist the subtitle promised.
6. Legend vs. record, inline, with relish. Honesty is part of the fun.
7. Audit every claim that isn't in the brief. The fluent sentences are the dangerous ones.
8. End on an image or a turn. Not a moral. Quieter is usually better.
9. One zinger per essay. One "refuse the obvious move" per essay, max.
10. Reread this file before the final pass, not just before the first draft.
