# The Worst Race Ever Run

The 1904 Olympic marathon is usually told as a comedy, and it's easy to see why. The winner was fed rat poison. The first man across the line had ridden most of the way in a car. A Cuban mailman stopped to eat fruit. A dog chased a runner into a cornfield. It's the kind of story that gets passed around on the internet with a headline like "The 1904 Olympic Marathon Was Absolutely Unhinged," and it deserves the headline. But I think the comedy hides something. The race was strange, yes, but it was strange on purpose.

## The setting

The 1904 Games weren't really an Olympics in the modern sense. They were a sideshow attached to the Louisiana Purchase Exposition, the enormous world's fair in St. Louis. The IOC had originally given the Games to Chicago, but St. Louis threatened to hold a rival competition, and the Games moved. They ran from July 1 to November 23 — nearly five months — and were spread so thinly across the fair that many athletes weren't sure if they'd competed in the Olympics or just in a fair event. Around 81% of the athletes were American. It was a home game.

The marathon started on the afternoon of August 30, at 3:03 pm, with the pistol fired by David R. Francis, the president of the fair. It was about 90 degrees in the shade, and humid. Thirty-two men started. The course was 40 kilometers, point to point, opening with five laps of the stadium track and then heading out onto country roads.

## The course

The roads were the problem. There were seven hills, between 100 and 300 feet. The surface was dust and broken stone. There was traffic: delivery wagons, trolleys, people walking their dogs. Officials and doctors drove alongside the runners in automobiles, and the cars kicked up clouds of dust that the runners had to breathe. One fair official later called it "the most difficult a human being was ever asked to run over."

And there was water at exactly one point on the course: a well, about twelve miles in.

This was not an accident. James E. Sullivan, the chief organizer of the Games, reportedly wanted to study what he called "purposeful dehydration." He wanted to see what would happen to men running in the heat without water.

He found out.

## The cast

**Fred Lorz**, a bricklayer from New York, cramped around mile nine. He climbed into one of the cars and rode along, waving at runners. When the car broke down around mile nineteen, he got out and jogged the rest of the way to the stadium, where he crossed the line first and was hailed as the winner. He was about to be given the gold medal when someone pointed out what had happened. Lorz said it was a joke. The Amateur Athletic Union banned him for life; after an apology, the ban was reduced to six months, and he won the Boston Marathon the next year.

**Thomas Hicks**, English-born but running for the United States, was the actual leader for much of the race. His handlers, Charles Lucas and Hugh McGrath, wouldn't let him drink — they sponged out his mouth with warm distilled water instead. About seven miles from the finish, they gave him a dose of strychnine mixed with egg white. Strychnine, at low doses, was thought of as a stimulant. It's also rat poison. When he faltered again, they gave him another dose, plus brandy and more eggs. Hicks began hallucinating — he thought the finish line was twenty miles away — and was half-carried over the line in 3 hours, 28 minutes and 53 seconds. It took four doctors about an hour to get him into a state where he could leave the stadium. He had lost about eight pounds.

Lucas, his handler, wrote about it later with real pride. He described Hicks running "mechanically — like a well-oiled piece of machinery."

**Félix Carvajal** was a Cuban, five foot one, and a former mailman. He'd raised money to get to the Games, then lost it gambling in New Orleans and hitchhiked the rest of the way. He showed up in a long-sleeved shirt, long trousers, a beret, and street shoes; someone cut his trousers off at the knee before the start. During the race he chatted with spectators and took peaches from people in a car. Legend says he also ate rotten apples from an orchard, got cramps, and lay down for a nap. He still finished fourth.

**Len Taunyane** and **Jan Mashiani** were two Tswana men from South Africa. They weren't at the fair as athletes. They had been hired, at four pounds a month, to perform in the fair's Boer War exhibition, which re-enacted battles for paying crowds. They were entered into the marathon, and they became the first Black Africans to compete in the Olympics. Taunyane finished ninth and Mashiani twelfth. One of them was chased off course by a dog.

**William Garcia**, from California, was found collapsed by the side of the road, bleeding internally from the dust he'd inhaled. He survived.

Of thirty-two starters, fourteen finished. It's still the worst finishing rate in Olympic marathon history, and the slowest winning time.

## The part that isn't funny

Two weeks before the marathon, on August 12 and 13, the fair held something called "Anthropology Days." The organizers took about a hundred Indigenous men who were being exhibited at the fair — from the Philippines, Africa, the Americas, Japan — and entered them in athletic events: running, jumping, pole climbing, mud fighting. The idea was to measure "primitive" athletic ability against white athletes. About 10,000 people watched. Taunyane ran in the "intertribal" marathon there and finished third.

Pierre de Coubertin, the founder of the modern Olympics, wasn't impressed by the whole thing, but not for the reasons you'd hope. He said the spectacle "will of course lose its appeal when black men, red men, and yellow men learn to run."

So the marathon wasn't an isolated bit of chaos. It took place inside a fair that was explicitly about measuring human bodies, ranking them, and putting them on display. The runners weren't just competitors. They were specimens. Sullivan's water experiment, the handlers treating Hicks like a machine to be tuned with chemicals, the South Africans brought in from a war re-enactment — all of it fits.

## Why we tell it as a comedy

The comic version of the story has grown over time. The rotten apples and the nap, for instance, don't appear in any contemporary source I can find; the earliest known version is from a 1948 history of the Olympics. The dog story might have been about the wrong runner. Some accounts say both South Africans ran barefoot; photographs suggest at least one wore shoes.

I don't think the embellishments are malicious. Stories get better when they're retold. But the comedy version does something convenient: it makes the race about individual weirdness — a cheater, a guy on drugs, a funny Cuban — instead of about the system that put them there.

## Conclusion

The 1904 marathon was the worst race ever run. But it wasn't bad because of bad luck. It was bad because the people running it were interested in finding out how much a human body could take, and they had a fair full of bodies to test. The real lesson isn't that early Olympics were silly. It's that they were serious about the wrong things.
