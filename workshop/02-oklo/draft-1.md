# The Missing Three Thousandths

In the spring of 1972, an analyst at a French fuel-processing plant ran a routine measurement on a uranium sample and got the wrong number. The sample should have been 0.7202% uranium-235. It came back 0.7171%.

That's a difference of 0.0031 percentage points. If you're not in the nuclear business, it sounds like a rounding error. If you are, it's a problem, because the share of U-235 in natural uranium is one of the most reliable numbers on Earth. It's the same in uranium from Canada, Australia, Kazakhstan, and moon rocks. It does not wander. And if a sample is missing U-235, the obvious explanation is that someone took it out.

The analyst was H. Bouzigues, at the Pierrelatte plant in southern France. What he'd found, after a few months of investigation, turned out to be a nuclear reactor. Not a French one. A natural one, two billion years old, buried in Gabon.

## Counting atoms

Nuclear materials accounting is not a glamorous job. Every gram of fissile material that passes through a facility is supposed to be tracked, because a few kilograms in the wrong hands make a bomb. So when the numbers don't add up, people pay attention.

The French Atomic Energy Commission (CEA) traced the low-U-235 uranium back to the Mounana mill near Franceville in Gabon, which was processing ore from a mine called Oklo. Depleted ore had been going out since 1970. By mid-1972, it added up to about 700 tons of uranium with around 200 kilograms of U-235 missing — enough, by one estimate, for about half a dozen bombs.

But the missing U-235 hadn't been stolen. Samples from the deposit contained fission products — the specific elements and isotopes you get when uranium atoms split. Some cores were down to 0.44% U-235. The uranium had been destroyed in place. Something had been fissioning it.

The CEA made the discovery public on September 25, 1972.

## How a rock becomes a reactor

The idea wasn't new. In 1956, a chemist at the University of Arkansas named Paul Kuroda published a two-page paper working out the conditions under which a uranium deposit could go critical on its own. You'd need a deposit thick enough — more than about two-thirds of a meter, the distance a fission neutron travels. You'd need enough U-235. You'd need a moderator, something to slow neutrons down so they can be captured by other uranium nuclei. And you'd need very few "neutron poisons," elements like boron that soak up neutrons.

Today, no uranium deposit can meet the second condition. Natural uranium is 0.72% U-235, and you can't make a reactor work with ordinary water as a moderator at that concentration; that's why reactor fuel is enriched to around 3-5%. But U-235 decays about six times faster than U-238, with a half-life of around 704 million years. Run the clock back two billion years and natural uranium was about 3% U-235. Roughly reactor grade.

At Oklo, the ore was unusually rich: 20-30% uranium in the reactor zones, compared to around 0.5% in the surrounding rock. And there was water — groundwater seeping through the deposit. Water is a moderator.

## The geyser

The most elegant part of Oklo is how it regulated itself. In 2004, Alex Meshik and colleagues at Washington University in St. Louis studied xenon isotopes trapped in mineral grains from one of the reactor zones and worked out the reactor's duty cycle. Groundwater would seep in, slow the neutrons, and the reaction would start. The heat would boil the water away. Without a moderator, the reaction would stop. The rock would cool, water would come back, and it would start again.

The cycle was about 30 minutes on and two and a half hours off. Like a geyser.

This went on for a long time — estimates range from around 150,000 years to several hundred thousand. Average power was modest, probably under 100 kilowatts, which Meshik compared to "a few dozen toasters." But over that time, the total output was around 15,000 megawatt-years. There were at least 16 separate reactor zones at Oklo and nearby Okélobondo, plus one more at Bangombé, about 35 km away.

## Why it matters

Oklo matters for at least two reasons.

The first is nuclear waste. The reactor zones made fission products and actinides, including more than two tons of plutonium, and then sat in wet rock for two billion years. Most of the heavy, non-volatile products moved only centimeters. That's encouraging for anyone designing a geological waste repository — although some elements, like cesium, iodine, and xenon, did migrate significantly, so it's not a perfect story.

The second is physics. Some nuclear reactions depend very sensitively on fundamental constants. In 1976, the Soviet physicist Alexander Shlyakhter realized that a samarium isotope at Oklo, which absorbs neutrons at a very specific energy, could be used to test whether those constants had changed. If the fine-structure constant — the number that governs the strength of electromagnetism — had been even slightly different two billion years ago, the samarium wouldn't have absorbed neutrons the same way. It had. In 1996, Thibault Damour and Freeman Dyson tightened the analysis: the fine-structure constant can't have changed by more than about one part in ten million over two billion years.

## Conclusion

Oklo is a reminder that nature got to nuclear fission first. It also shows how important careful measurement is: a three-thousandths of a percent discrepancy led to one of the most surprising discoveries in geology.
