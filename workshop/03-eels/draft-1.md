# Of the Tenderer Sex

In 1876, a nineteen-year-old medical student from Vienna spent a month in Trieste cutting open eels. He was looking for their testicles. He dissected more than four hundred of them and wrote home, a little plaintively: "All the eels I have cut open are of the tenderer sex."

The student was Sigmund Freud. He did eventually find something that might have been testes, in roughly the four-hundredth eel, but he couldn't be sure, and his paper on the subject was so hedged that he later left it off his list of publications. He went on to other things.

The eel didn't. It's still keeping the secret Freud was after.

## Mud

For most of history, nobody had ever seen an eel reproduce, because nobody had ever seen an eel egg, an eel sperm, a pregnant eel, or a baby eel that was recognizably an eel. Eels just showed up in rivers and ponds, grew fat, and at some point vanished.

Aristotle, who was usually a careful observer, concluded that they came from "the so-called earth's guts that grow spontaneously in mud and in humid ground." He said eels had been seen emerging from earthworms. Pliny thought they rubbed against rocks and the scraps came to life. Others suggested horsehair falling into water, or dew.

These theories sound silly but they were reasonable responses to the evidence. If something appears without any sign of having been born, spontaneous generation is the simplest explanation.

## The knife

The dissection era started in Italy. In the early 1700s a doctor from Comacchio, a lagoon town famous for its eel fisheries, found ovaries in an unusually large eel. Carlo Mondini described them properly in 1777, establishing that eels were ordinary fish with ordinary female organs. But the males were missing.

In 1874, a Polish zoologist named Szymon Syrski claimed to have found the testes — a lobed organ that was named after him. It was this claim that Freud was sent to check, and couldn't confirm.

The problem, it later became clear, was that the eels in rivers aren't sexually mature. Their gonads only develop on the way to spawning, which happens somewhere far away.

## The net

The next breakthrough was larval. A small, transparent, leaf-shaped fish called *Leptocephalus brevirostris* had been described as its own species. In 1886, a French zoologist kept some alive in an aquarium until they turned into eels. In 1896, Giovanni Battista Grassi, working with larvae caught in the Strait of Messina, traced the whole metamorphosis. The leaf was a baby eel.

Since the larvae were found in the Mediterranean, people assumed eels spawned there. Then, in 1904, a Danish research ship caught an eel larva west of the Faroe Islands, in the Atlantic. Johannes Schmidt, the Danish biologist on board, realized that if he could find where the larvae were smallest, he'd find where they were born.

He spent nearly two decades doing it, crossing the Atlantic with nets, funded largely by the Carlsberg brewery's foundation. The larvae got smaller and smaller as he went west. In 1921–22, he found the smallest ones ever seen, in the Sargasso Sea, a region of the western Atlantic bounded by currents rather than land. That was where European eels were born.

## The tag

Schmidt's conclusion was right, as far as anyone can tell, but it was an inference. No one had ever caught an adult eel in the Sargasso Sea, or found an egg. That stayed true for another hundred years.

In 2022, a team led by Rosalind Wright at the UK's Cefas published the results of a satellite tracking study. They had tagged 26 large silver eels — mature migrants — and released them off the Azores. The tags were designed to pop off and transmit after a set time. Five of them surfaced inside the Sargasso Sea, and one inside the presumed spawning area. Four eels dove so deep that their tags released automatically.

It was the first direct evidence that European eels actually make the journey. It still wasn't spawning. As the paper put it, "surveys have failed to locate either adult eels or eggs" in the Sargasso.

## The cost of not knowing

This would be a charming story about the limits of science if not for the fact that European eels are critically endangered. The number of glass eels — the transparent juveniles that arrive in European rivers — is down about 95% from the 1960s and 70s.

Part of the reason is trafficking. Glass eels can sell for up to €6,000 per kilogram in Asia, where they're raised in farms and sold as legal fish. Europol estimates up to 100 tonnes a year are smuggled out of Europe.

And they have to be wild-caught, because nobody can breed eels in captivity. The life cycle has never been closed: the best anyone has done is keep captive-bred larvae alive for about 200 days. We can't farm them because we don't know how they reproduce. The mystery isn't just academic. It's the reason the species can't be rescued by aquaculture.

## The well

In 1859, an eight-year-old boy in the Swedish fishing village of Brantevik dropped an eel into his family's well. It was still there in 2014, when the owner of the house found it dead. If the story is true, it was 155 years old. Experts are skeptical — 155 is "quite a stretch," one said — but eels kept away from the sea do seem to live a very long time. An eel named Putte lived 85 years in a Swedish aquarium.

The theory is that an eel that never gets to start its migration never matures, and so never dies the way eels are supposed to die, after spawning. It just waits.

## Conclusion

The eel has outlasted every method we've used to understand it: speculation, dissection, nets, and satellites. Each one got us closer. None got us all the way. Hopefully we figure it out before the eels are gone.
