Here is the most famous meal in the history of finance.

Sometime in the 1630s, a sailor arrives in Holland carrying news for a wealthy merchant. The merchant, pleased, rewards him with "a fine red herring for his breakfast." On his way out, the sailor notices what looks like an onion lying on the merchant's counter, among the silks and velvets. He pockets it, thinking it'll go nicely with the herring. He's later found sitting on a coil of rope, finishing his breakfast. The onion was a bulb of the Semper Augustus tulip, which according to the story could have "sumptuously feasted the Prince of Orange and the whole court of the Stadtholder." The sailor goes to jail.

This comes from Charles Mackay's *Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds*, published in 1841, which is where most people's idea of tulip mania ultimately comes from. Mackay tells us that in the 1630s the Dutch, "even to its lowest dregs, embarked in the tulip trade." He tells us bulbs were swapped for estates. He prints a list of goods — two lasts of wheat, four fat oxen, a thousand pounds of cheese, a complete bed, a silver drinking cup — that one Viceroy bulb was supposedly traded for. And he tells us that when the bubble burst in 1637, it left behind ruin: "many who, for a brief season, had emerged from the humbler walks of life, were cast back into their original obscurity."

Every finance student hears some version of this. It's in textbooks, it's invoked every time Bitcoin doubles, and it's become shorthand for a kind of collective insanity.

The trouble is that almost none of it seems to have happened.

## The archive

In the 2000s, the historian Anne Goldgar went looking for the victims. She spent years in Dutch archives — notarial records, wills, small-claims court files — in Haarlem, Amsterdam, Alkmaar, and Enkhuizen, looking for the people who'd traded tulips and what happened to them. Her book, *Tulipmania: Money, Honor, and Knowledge in the Dutch Golden Age*, came out in 2007.

She identified about 350 people involved in the trade. They weren't chimney sweeps and weavers. They were mostly well-off merchants and skilled artisans, people who could afford to gamble. Only 37 of them ever paid more than 300 guilders for a bulb.

And the ruin? "I couldn't find anybody that went bankrupt," she told Smithsonian. There were a handful of people in financial trouble around that time, but she couldn't connect their problems to tulips. The painter Jan van Goyen, sometimes cited as a tulip casualty, did die bankrupt — in 1656, nineteen years later, mostly thanks to land speculation.

So where did the story come from?

## The pamphlets

Mackay's main source was an 18th-century German writer, Johann Beckmann, whose account traced back through the botanist Abraham Munting to a set of Dutch pamphlets printed right after the crash in 1637. The most famous was a dialogue between two characters, Waermondt and Gaergoedt — roughly "True-mouth" and "Greedy-goods" — published in Haarlem by Adriaen Roman.

These were moral tracts. They were written to warn people off speculation as a sin, and they used vivid, exaggerated stories to do it. Goldgar calls them propaganda, and notes that later writers cited them "as if [they] were fact." Most of the price data in later accounts, including the eye-popping ones, came from these pamphlets — specifically, from the very last days of trading.

The sailor and the onion, the beggared weavers, the whole country losing its mind: they come from a sermon, retold by an encyclopedist, retold by a Victorian journalist who was writing a book about how gullible people are.

## What actually happened

Some things are documented.

Tulips were genuinely valuable. The most prized were "broken" tulips, with feathered, flame-like streaks of color. We now know the streaks were caused by a virus spread by aphids; it also weakened the bulbs, so the most beautiful varieties reproduced slowly. Rare bulbs were rare for a reason. The Semper Augustus was one of them, and it really did fetch prices around 5,500 guilders in 1637.

There was a real trading scene. From the summer of 1636, traders met in taverns in groups called "colleges," buying and selling contracts for future delivery of bulbs that were still in the ground. The Dutch called it *windhandel*, the wind trade, because no bulbs changed hands.

There was a real crash. In the first week of February 1637, starting in Haarlem, buyers vanished. A contemporary satire says it began on February 3, at an auction where nobody would bid even as the price was cut.

And there was real damage, but of a different kind than Mackay describes. Since most trades were contracts for future delivery, the crash meant buyers refused to pay. Courts mostly declined to enforce the contracts. Paper losses largely stayed on paper. What broke, in Goldgar's telling, was trust: a society that ran on reputation discovered that a lot of respectable people would back out of deals.

## The economists' version

Economists have their own revision. Peter Garber, in a 1989 paper and later in *Famous First Bubbles*, argued that the prices of rare bulbs weren't crazy. A new, rare variety of flower is valuable for the same reason any new, scarce asset is valuable. Its price is then expected to fall as it multiplies. He found that rare tulip prices in the following years fell about as fast as rare bulb prices normally did in the 18th century.

He did concede that one part was hard to explain: in January 1637, the price of common bulbs, sold by weight, shot up something like twentyfold and then collapsed. His explanation was that it was a "meaningless winter drinking game" by people in the middle of a plague.

Earl Thompson, in 2007, offered another explanation: the crazy final prices were an artifact of a legal change. Contracts that had been futures were being converted, in effect, into options, which meant buyers could walk away by paying a small fee. If you can buy something at a high price but only pay a fraction if prices fall, you'll happily sign at a high price.

## The real bubble

So tulip mania was a small, real speculative episode among relatively wealthy people, with a sharp spike at the end that might be explained by contract rules, followed by a lot of broken promises and not much actual ruin.

The thing that got wildly overvalued was the story. A moral pamphlet became a German history, which became a Victorian bestseller, which became an economics textbook, each time losing its sourcing and gaining certainty. Mackay's book is about the madness of crowds. It's also an example of it.
